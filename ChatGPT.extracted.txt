Revised Educational Philosophy
The audience has three characteristics that should drive every decision:
1. They are geologists/geophysicists first
Their existing mental models are likely based on:
rocks 
minerals 
structures 
maps 
sections 
geochemical anomalies 
geophysical signatures 
drill holes 
interpretation 
exploration decisions 
We should use those concepts as the bridge into AI.
For example, don't begin with:
“Machine learning is a subset of artificial intelligence in which algorithms learn patterns from data…”
Instead:
“Imagine you have thousands of drill-hole intervals where experienced geologists have already identified the lithology. Could a computer learn the characteristics that distinguish those lithologies and then help classify new intervals?”
Then introduce:
This is supervised machine learning.
The terminology comes after the geological idea.
2. AI is not their first priority
This is extremely important.
We should not try to teach them everything:
Python programming 
linear algebra 
calculus 
probability theory 
neural-network mathematics 
advanced statistics 
TensorFlow/PyTorch 
computer vision architectures 
advanced GIS 
advanced database engineering 
Those would consume the workshop without achieving the main objective.
Instead, we teach them enough to become AI-literate exploration geologists.
Their learning progression should be:
Awareness → Understanding → Application → Critical Evaluation → Career Direction
not:
Programming → Algorithms → Mathematics → AI
3. They need a strong foundation before ML
I would therefore expand the introduction and data foundations.
The revised five-hour structure should be:
This is a better fit for beginners.
MODULE 1
Mineral Exploration in the Age of AI
40 minutes
The objective is not to teach AI yet.
The objective is to make students care.
Opening scenario
I would open with a question:
“You are the exploration geologist responsible for 1,000 km² of ground. You have limited money and can drill only 10 holes. Where do you drill?”
Then show them progressively:
Stage 1
A geological map.
Ask:
Where would you investigate?
Stage 2
Add geochemistry.
Ask:
Does your decision change?
Stage 3
Add magnetics.
Does your interpretation change again?
Stage 4
Add structures.
Stage 5
Add remote sensing.
Stage 6
Add historical drilling.
Now they are dealing with a multi-dimensional exploration problem.
Then ask:
“What if you had 50 million measurements?”
That's the reason AI enters the story.
The fundamental exploration problem
Students should understand that exploration is fundamentally about:
Finding patterns
Combining evidence
Reducing uncertainty
Making decisions
Testing hypotheses
AI can potentially assist with all five.
What changed in modern exploration?
We explain the progression:
Traditional exploration
Geologist:
Observe → Interpret → Decide
Modern exploration
Geologist:
Observe → Collect data → Integrate datasets → Analyse → Interpret → Decide
Data-rich exploration
Geologist + computational tools:
Observe → Collect → Integrate → Analyse → Model → Predict → Interpret → Validate → Decide
The human geological interpretation remains central.
Introduce AI carefully
Only after establishing the problem:
Artificial Intelligence is a broad term for computer systems designed to perform tasks that normally require aspects of human intelligence.
Then:
Machine Learning is an approach where computers learn patterns from examples/data rather than being explicitly programmed with every rule.
Then:
Deep Learning is a family of machine-learning methods based on neural networks that can learn complex representations, particularly from large datasets such as images, signals and sequences.
That's enough at this stage.
Important distinction
Students should immediately understand:
AI ≠ ML
ML ≠ Deep Learning
AI ≠ ChatGPT
And:
AI ≠ automatically intelligent geology
This distinction will prevent many misconceptions later.
Activity 1 — “Does this need AI?”
Give students exploration tasks.
For each one:
A. Useful application of AI
B. Possible application but not necessarily necessary
C. AI is probably not the appropriate solution
Examples:
Automatically identify lithology from core photographs 
Calculate the average grade of a dataset 
Find duplicate drill-hole records 
Identify geochemical anomalies 
Interpret a complex geological setting 
Convert metres to feet 
Predict mineral prospectivity 
Decide whether a mining project is economically viable 
The purpose isn't getting every answer correct.
It's teaching:
AI should solve a problem, not exist for its own sake.

MODULE 3
AI & Machine Learning Without the Jargon
60 minutes
Now the students are ready.
This module should answer:
“What actually happens when we give geological data to a machine-learning model?”
Start with human learning
Suppose students receive 100 examples of:
granite 
basalt 
shale 
limestone 
They begin noticing:
colour 
texture 
mineralogy 
grain size 
density 
magnetic properties 
Eventually they can classify a new sample.
Machine learning is conceptually similar:
Examples
   ↓
Patterns
   ↓
Model
   ↓
New example
   ↓
Prediction
Introduce features
A feature is simply:
A measurable piece of information used by the model.
For a lithology model:
density 
magnetic susceptibility 
elemental concentrations 
alteration indicators 
For a prospectivity model:
distance to structure 
Au anomaly 
As anomaly 
lithology 
magnetic response 
alteration 
Introduce labels
A label is:
The known answer we want the model to learn to predict.
Example:
Features:
Au + As + structure + lithology
Label:
Known mineralisation / no known mineralisation
Supervised learning
Explain using geology.
KNOWN EXAMPLES
Geological features ─────→ Known outcome
       │                        │
       │                        ↓
       └──────────────→ ML Model
                              │
                              ↓
                     Unknown location
                              │
                              ↓
                         Prediction
Classification
Example:
What lithology is this?
Output:
Granite / Basalt / Shale / etc.
Regression
Example:
What is the predicted value?
Output:
Au = 0.73 ppm
or perhaps a predicted continuous geological/geochemical property.
Clustering
Now remove the labels.
Ask:
“Can we find groups without telling the algorithm what the groups are?”
This introduces unsupervised learning.
Then make the crucial point:
A machine-learning cluster is not automatically a geological domain.
Anomaly detection
Show:
Normal population

                      •

Ask:
What might those unusual observations represent?
Possibilities:
mineralisation 
contamination 
analytical error 
unusual lithology 
sampling problem 
genuinely interesting geology 
This is an excellent place to teach critical thinking.
Decision trees
I would definitely introduce decision trees because they are extremely intuitive for this audience.
Example:
              Au anomaly?
              /          \
            Yes           No
            /              \
     Near structure?       Low
       /      \
     Yes       No
      ↓         ↓
   High       Medium
 prospectivity
Then explain:
This is conceptually similar to how a geologist might construct a decision process.
Random forests
Explain conceptually rather than mathematically:
Instead of relying on one decision tree, combine many trees and aggregate their predictions.
No need to derive bagging.
Neural networks
Students should understand the idea, not the mathematics.
Use:
INPUTS
Pixels / signals / measurements
        ↓
     Layers
        ↓
Learned patterns
        ↓
Prediction
Then explain why they are powerful for:
images 
remote sensing 
core photographs 
geophysical signals 
But also explain that they generally require appropriate data and careful validation.
What about ChatGPT?
Because students will inevitably associate AI with generative AI, explain:
Generative AI
Examples:
text generation 
image generation 
coding assistants 
document analysis 
Predictive ML
Examples:
prospectivity prediction 
lithology classification 
grade prediction 
anomaly detection 
These are related through the broader AI field but solve different types of problems.
Very important:
“AI doesn't discover gold.”
It can:
identify patterns associated with known mineralisation.
It can:
prioritize targets.
It can:
classify geological information.
It can:
assist interpretation.
It can:
automate repetitive work.
But it cannot guarantee:
“There is an economic deposit here.”
ML workflow
Students should memorize this—not equations:
1. Define problem
       ↓
2. Collect data
       ↓
3. Understand data
       ↓
4. Clean data
       ↓
5. Prepare features
       ↓
6. Split data
       ↓
7. Train model
       ↓
8. Test model
       ↓
9. Interpret results
       ↓
10. Geological validation
       ↓
11. Exploration decision
This becomes the backbone of the rest of the workshop.
MODULE 4
Complete AI-Assisted Exploration Workflow
60 minutes
This should be the main practical learning experience.
Case Study
“Where Should We Drill Next?”
We will create a fictional but geologically realistic exploration project.
Target commodity
Gold
Geological setting
A simplified orogenic gold exploration environment.
The students are given:
regional geology 
structures 
Au geochemistry 
pathfinder elements 
magnetic data 
alteration information 
historical drilling 
known mineralised occurrences 
Step 1 — Geological question
Where are the areas most prospective for gold mineralisation?
Step 2 — Geological hypothesis
Students identify potential controls:
structural corridors 
favourable lithologies 
alteration 
geochemical anomalies 
geophysical response 
Step 3 — Data integration
Bring the datasets together.
This is an important concept:
AI becomes useful when we can represent multiple evidence sources together.
Step 4 — Data preparation
Show intentionally messy data.
For example:
Sample     Au      As       Lithology      X        Y
A001       0.03    15       Basalt         ...      ...
A002       0.05    21       basalt         ...      ...
A003       -       18       BAS            ...      ...
A004       9999    17       Basalt         ...      ...
Ask students:
Would you give this directly to an ML model?
Absolutely not.
Then identify:
missing values 
inconsistent codes 
suspicious values 
unit problems 
detection limits 
Step 5 — Feature engineering
This is an important but beginner-friendly concept.
Instead of simply using:
X and Y
we can derive:
distance to structure 
geochemical anomaly score 
alteration indicator 
lithological favourability 
magnetic anomaly 
pathfinder association 
Explain:
Feature engineering means transforming raw information into variables that better represent the problem we want the model to solve.
This is where geology becomes extremely valuable.
Step 6 — Train the model
Use a relatively interpretable model first.
For teaching purposes, I would demonstrate:
Random Forest
rather than jumping directly into deep learning.
Why?
Because students can understand:
Many decision trees → combined prediction
without needing advanced mathematics.
Step 7 — Evaluate
Show:
confusion matrix 
accuracy 
precision/recall 
ROC-AUC conceptually 
But then immediately challenge the metric.
Ask:
“Is 95% accuracy necessarily a good exploration model?”
Students should eventually answer:
No.
Step 8 — Produce prospectivity map
Convert model output into:
Low → Medium → High prospectivity
Now the students finally see the payoff.
Step 9 — Geological interpretation
This is where the geologist comes back in.
Ask:
Does the high-prospectivity area:
coincide with structures? 
occur in favourable rocks? 
have supporting geochemistry? 
have sufficient data? 
have reasonable geophysical evidence? 
resemble known mineralisation? 
represent interpolation or extrapolation? 
Step 10 — Drill decision
Give students a budget:
Only 3 holes can be drilled.
Let them choose.
Step 11 — Reveal new information
This is where the case study becomes powerful.
One target:
High AI score + excellent geological support
One target:
High AI score + poor geological support
One target:
Moderate AI score + excellent geological evidence
Ask:
Which three holes would you drill?
This teaches the central message:
AI output is evidence—not the decision.

MODULE 6
Can We Trust AI?
30 minutes
I would make this short but powerful.
The students have just seen AI produce a prospectivity map.
Now we challenge it.
The “92% Prospectivity” Problem
Show:
Target A — AI prospectivity: 92%
Ask:
“Would you drill it?”
Then progressively reveal:
Problem 1
Almost all training samples came from known mineralised areas.
Problem 2
The target is outside the geological conditions represented in the training data.
Problem 3
There is very little data in that area.
Problem 4
The model was randomly split rather than spatially validated.
Problem 5
One important feature contains leakage.
Now ask again:
“Would you drill it?”
This creates a much deeper understanding than simply listing model limitations.
The five questions every geologist should ask an AI model
I would give students a memorable framework:
1. What data did you learn from?
2. Is that data representative?
3. How was the model tested?
4. Why did it make this prediction?
5. Does the prediction make geological sense?
If students remember these five questions, the workshop has achieved a major objective.

Revised Technical Depth
For this particular audience, I would roughly target:
60% Geological concepts & applications
20% AI/ML concepts
10% Data/statistics
5% programming demonstrations
5% mathematics
These aren't rigid percentages; they're a design guide.
The programming and mathematics should support understanding rather than become independent subjects.
What I Would Explicitly NOT Teach
Given only five hours and the students' background, I would avoid detailed treatment of:

The Desired Student Experience
At the beginning:
“AI isn't really my field.”
After 1 hour:
“Okay, I understand why exploration generates data.”
After 2 hours:
“I understand roughly how machine learning learns patterns.”
After 3–4 hours:
“I can see how ML could actually help with exploration targeting.”
After 4.5 hours:
“But I also understand why an AI prediction can be wrong.”
At the end:
“I don't need to become an AI specialist, but I need enough AI/data literacy to work effectively with these technologies as a future geologist or geophysicist.”
That is the educational outcome I would target.
