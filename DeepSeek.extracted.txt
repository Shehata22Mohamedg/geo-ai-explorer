PHASE 1: ARCHITECTURE OVERVIEW
Design Philosophy
This workshop is designed for students who:
Have strong geological foundations but little to no AI/ML knowledge
May be skeptical or anxious about technology
Need to understand why this matters for their careers
Learn best through geological examples, not abstract concepts
Core Teaching Principles:
Geology First, AI Second – Every concept starts with a geological problem
Intuition Before Terminology – Build understanding before introducing jargon
Show, Don't Just Tell – Visual demonstrations and activities throughout
Honest About Limitations – AI is powerful but flawed; critical thinking is essential
Career-Relevant – Connect everything to their future professional lives

PHASE 2: DETAILED CONTENT PLAN
MODULE 1: Why Should a Geologist Care About AI? (45 minutes)
Opening (5 minutes)
Hook: Start with a provocative question:
"In the next 10 years, will you be replaced by a computer? The answer is no – but the geologist who knows how to work WITH computers might replace the geologist who doesn't."
Brief personal introduction – Establish credibility as someone who has worked in both exploration and data science.
Section 1.1: The Exploration Challenge (10 minutes)
The Problem We Face:
Discovery rates are declining globally despite increasing exploration spending
Deposits are getting deeper, more complex, and harder to find
We collect more data than ever before – but how do we use it all?
"Brownfields" vs. "Greenfields" exploration challenges
Key Statistics to Share:
A typical exploration project generates terabytes of data
A single drill hole can produce thousands of geochemical measurements
Regional geophysical surveys cover thousands of square kilometers
No human can visually integrate all this data effectively
Discussion Question:
"How many of you have looked at a geochemical dataset with 50 elements and 5,000 samples? How did you make sense of it?"
Section 1.2: What is AI and Machine Learning? (15 minutes)
Simple Definition:
Artificial Intelligence (AI): The broad field of making computers do things that normally require human intelligence
Machine Learning (ML): A subset of AI where computers learn patterns from data without being explicitly programmed
Deep Learning: A type of ML using neural networks with many layers (we'll touch on this)
The Key Difference – Traditional Programming vs. Machine Learning:
Traditional Programming:
You write rules: "IF copper > 1000 ppm AND magnetic_low = TRUE THEN drill_target = TRUE"
Problem: Geology is too complex for simple rules
Machine Learning:
You show examples: "Here are 500 locations where we found mineralization, and 500 where we didn't"
The computer figures out the rules itself
It can find patterns too complex for humans to see
Geological Analogy:
"Think of it like training a junior geologist. You don't give them a rulebook that says 'drill here.' You take them to the field, show them outcrops, show them drill core, and let them develop intuition. Machine learning is similar – we show the computer examples until it learns the patterns."
The Critical Point:
"The computer doesn't understand geology. It finds statistical patterns. Our job is to interpret those patterns geologically – and to know when they make sense and when they don't."
Section 1.3: What Problems Can AI Solve in Exploration? (15 minutes)
Four Main Categories:
1. Classification: "What is this?"
Geological example: Identify rock types from drill core photos
Geological example: Classify alteration minerals from hyperspectral data
Geological example: Determine if a geochemical sample is anomalous or background
2. Regression/Prediction: "How much is there?"
Geological example: Predict gold grade from pathfinder elements
Geological example: Estimate depth to basement from geophysical data
Geological example: Predict porosity from seismic attributes
3. Clustering/Anomaly Detection: "What's different here?"
Geological example: Group soil samples into geochemical populations
Geological example: Identify unusual geophysical signatures
Geological example: Find samples that don't fit any known pattern
4. Image Analysis: "What do I see in this picture?"
Geological example: Automate logging of drill core photographs
Geological example: Detect lineaments from satellite imagery
Geological example: Identify mineral grains in thin section images
Interactive Activity (5 minutes):
"Turn to your neighbor. Think of a task you've done in a geology class or field camp that was repetitive or time-consuming. Could AI help with that? How?"
Possible answers:
Measuring grains in thin section
Describing hundreds of meters of drill core
Picking first breaks on seismic data
Recording structural measurements
Section 1.4: AI as a Tool, Not a Replacement (5 minutes)
Key Message:
"AI is like a very powerful, very fast, but very literal research assistant. It can process more data than you ever could, but it has no geological intuition. It doesn't know what makes sense. It doesn't understand the concept of a 'deposit.' It only knows patterns."
The Human-AI Partnership:
AI does: Process large datasets, find subtle patterns, rank targets, automate repetitive tasks
Geologists do: Define the problem, interpret results, validate against geological knowledge, make decisions, take responsibility
Closing Thought for Module 1:
"You are not becoming obsolete. You are gaining a new tool. But to use it well, you need to understand what it can and cannot do. That's what the rest of this workshop is about."
MODULE 2: The Data We Work With – Preparing for AI (60 minutes)
Introduction (3 minutes)
"Machine learning is powerful, but it has one golden rule: Garbage In, Garbage Out. If your data is bad, your model will be bad – no matter how sophisticated the algorithm. In this module, we'll look at the data we work with and what it takes to prepare it for AI."
Section 2.1: The Data Smorgasbord (15 minutes)
Overview of Exploration Data Types:
1. Geochemistry
Soil samples, rock chips, stream sediments, drill core assays
Multi-element data (often 30-60 elements analyzed)
Units: ppm, ppb, wt%, g/t
Issues: Detection limits, censored data, different labs, different digestions
Example: A soil survey with 5,000 samples analyzed for 50 elements = 250,000 data points
2. Geophysics
Magnetic, gravity, radiometric, electromagnetic, IP, seismic
Gridded data (regular spacing) vs. point data (irregular)
Different resolutions and depths of investigation
Issues: Different survey parameters, merging datasets, removing cultural noise
Example: A regional magnetic survey covering 10,000 km² at 200m line spacing = 50,000 km of data
3. Geology
Maps (polygons, lines, points)
Structural measurements (strike/dip, foliation)
Lithological logs (qualitative descriptions)
Alteration logs
Issues: Subjective descriptions, inconsistent terminology, scale differences
4. Remote Sensing
Multispectral (Landsat, ASTER) – broad mineral groups
Hyperspectral (Hyperion, airborne) – specific minerals
Radar (for topography and structure)
Issues: Cloud cover, vegetation, atmospheric correction
5. Drill Core Data
Photographs (visual logging)
Geotechnical measurements
Downhole geophysics
Issues: Variable photo quality, lighting, orientation
6. Spatial Data
Coordinates (latitude/longitude, UTM)
Projections and datum
GIS layers
Issues: Different coordinate systems, spatial accuracy
Interactive Question:
"Which of these data types have you worked with? Which ones do you think are easiest or hardest to prepare for AI?"
Section 2.2: The Golden Rule – Garbage In, Garbage Out (10 minutes)
A Cautionary Tale:
"I once worked on a project where a single sample had a copper value of 50,000 ppm. The geologist assumed it was a typo – should have been 500 ppm. They 'corrected' it and moved on. But what if that 50,000 ppm was real? What if they just threw away the highest-grade sample in the entire dataset? This is the kind of error that can destroy a project."
Types of Data Problems:
1. Missing Values
Some samples not analyzed for all elements
Some drill holes not logged for all features
Options: Delete the sample, delete the feature, or impute (fill in) the missing value
Geological consideration: Why is the data missing? Is it random or systematic?
2. Outliers
Values that are unusually high or low
Geological question: Is this an error, or is it a real anomaly?
Key insight: In mineral exploration, we're often LOOKING for outliers!
3. Inconsistent Data
Different labs, different detection limits
Different geologists, different logging styles
Different surveys, different parameters
Solution: Standardization and documentation
4. Censored Data
Values below detection limit reported as "<5 ppm"
Common in geochemistry for trace elements
Options: Replace with detection limit/2, use statistical methods for censored data
Geological consideration: What does "below detection" mean for this element?
Activity: Spot the Problems (5 minutes)
Show a small sample dataset (10-15 rows) with:
Missing values
An obvious outlier
Inconsistent units
A censored value
Ask students: "What problems do you see? What would you do about each?"
Section 2.3: The 80/20 Rule – Data Preparation Dominates (10 minutes)
The Reality:
"In real-world machine learning projects, data preparation takes 70-80% of the time. The actual modeling – choosing an algorithm, training, testing – is the easy part. This is why data-savvy geologists are so valuable – they understand the data."
Data Preparation Steps:
1. Cleaning
Remove duplicates
Fix typos and obvious errors
Standardize units and terminology
Handle missing values
2. Transformation
Log-transform geochemical data (most elements are log-normally distributed)
Normalize or standardize data (so all features contribute equally)
Create derived features (e.g., ratios like Cu/Mo, or distance to nearest fault)
3. Quality Control (QA/QC)
Standards: Known composition samples inserted into sample stream
Blanks: Empty samples to detect contamination
Duplicates: Same sample analyzed twice to check precision
Why this matters for AI: If you can't trust your data, you can't trust your model
4. Spatial Considerations
Project data to a common coordinate system
Check for spatial bias in sampling
Consider spatial autocorrelation (nearby samples are similar)
The "Clinched" Dataset Problem:
"Imagine all your soil samples were collected along a road. Your AI model will learn to find deposits near roads. But what about the deposit 5 km from the nearest road? The model might miss it entirely because it has no examples to learn from."
Visual: Show a map with sample points clustered along a road, with a large unsampled area.
Section 2.4: The Data Leakage Trap (7 minutes)
What is Data Leakage?
"Data leakage is when your model accidentally gets access to information it shouldn't have – information that wouldn't be available when you actually use the model to make predictions. It's like cheating on a test by looking at the answer key."
Geological Example:
"You want to build a model to predict where to drill next. You train it using all your drill hole data, including the final grade of each hole. Then you use this model to predict which undrilled areas are most prospective. But wait – you used the grade (the answer) to train the model. The model has learned to predict grade from grade. This is leakage."
Correct Approach:
"You can only use information that would be available BEFORE you drill. So you might use geophysics, surface geochemistry, and geological mapping to predict where mineralization might be. You would NOT use the drill hole grades as input features."
Another Example:
"You're building a model to predict rock type from drill core photos. You train it on photos where you already know the rock type. That's fine – the rock type is the label you're trying to predict. But you can't use the rock type as an input feature – that would be leakage."
Key Takeaway:
"Always ask: Would I have this information at the time I want to make a prediction? If not, it's leakage."
Section 2.5: Spatial Bias – The Hidden Problem (5 minutes)
The Issue:
"Exploration data is almost never randomly distributed. We sample where it's easy: near roads, near outcrop, near old mines. This creates spatial bias that can seriously affect AI models."
Visual Examples:
Sample points along a road
Sample points clustered around old workings
Sample points only in areas with good outcrop (no cover)
Why This Matters:
"A model trained on biased data will be biased. It will be good at finding deposits in areas similar to where it was trained, but it may completely fail in new areas."
Mitigation Strategies:
Be aware of the bias
Test the model on spatially separate data (not just random split)
Use cross-validation by region, not by random sample
Collect new data in underrepresented areas
Module 2 Summary (5 minutes)
Key Points:
Exploration involves diverse data types: geochemistry, geophysics, geology, remote sensing, drilling
Data quality is paramount – Garbage In, Garbage Out
Data preparation takes 70-80% of the effort
Watch out for: missing values, outliers, censored data, spatial bias, data leakage
QA/QC is not optional – it's essential
Closing Thought:
"The geologist who understands data is more valuable than ever. Your geological knowledge helps you identify problems that a pure data scientist would miss. You are the quality control for the AI."
MODULE 3: How Machine Learning Actually Works (75 minutes)
Introduction (3 minutes)
"Now we get to the engine room. But don't worry – we're not going to derive equations. We're going to build intuition using geological examples. By the end of this module, you'll understand what machine learning models actually do, and you'll be able to evaluate whether they're working well or not."
Section 3.1: The Machine Learning Workflow (10 minutes)
The Basic Steps:
Step 1: Collect and Prepare Data
We covered this in Module 2
You need examples (samples, locations) with features (measurements) and labels (what you want to predict)
Step 2: Split the Data
Training set (70-80%): Used to teach the model
Testing set (20-30%): Used to evaluate the model
Why split? To see if the model can generalize to new data it hasn't seen before
Step 3: Choose a Model
Different algorithms for different problems
Classification, regression, clustering
We'll look at a few key types
Step 4: Train the Model
The algorithm finds patterns in the training data
It adjusts its internal parameters to minimize errors
Step 5: Evaluate the Model
Test on the held-out testing set
How accurate is it? What types of errors does it make?
Step 6: Use the Model
Apply to new data to make predictions
But always with geological validation!
Visual: A flowchart showing this process
Geological Analogy:
"Think of training a model like training a geologist. You show them many examples of mineralized and barren rocks. They learn the patterns. Then you take them to a new area and ask them to make predictions. If they learned well, they'll do well. If they just memorized the training examples, they'll fail."
Section 3.2: Supervised vs. Unsupervised Learning (7 minutes)
Supervised Learning:
You have labeled examples
You know the "right answer" for each example
Goal: Learn to predict the label from the features
Geological Examples:
Classification: "This drill core sample is mineralized" (yes/no)
Classification: "This rock is a basalt" (rock type)
Regression: "This sample contains 1.2 g/t gold" (number)
Unsupervised Learning:
No labels
Goal: Find structure or patterns in the data
Geological Examples:
Clustering: "Group these 1,000 soil samples into 5 geochemical populations"
Anomaly detection: "Find the samples that don't fit any known pattern"
Visual: Side-by-side comparison
Key Question:
"When would you use each? If you're trying to predict something you can measure (like grade), use supervised learning. If you're trying to discover something you don't know (like how many geochemical populations exist), use unsupervised learning."
Section 3.3: Demonstration 1 – The "Rock Detective" (k-Nearest Neighbors) (15 minutes)
Setup:
Show a scatter plot with two axes: Zr (ppm) vs. TiO2 (wt%)
Plot points for three known rock types: Basalt (blue), Andesite (green), Rhyolite (red)
Each point represents a sample with known rock type
Part 1: Visual Classification (5 minutes)
"I'm going to show you a new sample – the big yellow star. Based on its position on this plot, what rock type do you think it is? Turn to your neighbor and discuss for 30 seconds."
Students will likely say: "Andesite, because it's closest to the green points."
"Exactly! You just did classification. You looked at the neighbors of the unknown sample and predicted its type based on the majority class of those neighbors."
Part 2: How k-NN Works (5 minutes)
"The k-Nearest Neighbors algorithm works exactly the same way. 'k' is the number of neighbors you look at."
Show the same plot with circles around the unknown sample:
k=1: Look at only the closest neighbor
k=3: Look at the 3 closest neighbors
k=5: Look at the 5 closest neighbors
"If k=1, the prediction is whatever the closest sample is. If k=5, you take a vote – the most common class among the 5 nearest neighbors wins."
Visual: Show how the prediction might change with different values of k
Part 3: Why This Matters (5 minutes)
"k-NN is simple, but it illustrates important concepts:
The model learns from examples
Predictions are based on similarity
The choice of 'k' matters – too small and you're sensitive to noise, too large and you might miss local patterns
In geology, this is like saying 'this sample is similar to these other samples, so it's probably the same rock type'"
Extension:
"Now imagine this plot has not 2 dimensions but 50 – one for each element in a geochemical analysis. The computer can find neighbors in 50-dimensional space, which is impossible for us to visualize. That's the power of machine learning."
Section 3.4: Demonstration 2 – Finding Hidden Groups (k-Means Clustering) (15 minutes)
Setup:
Show a scatter plot of soil samples with no labels
Two geochemical variables (e.g., Cu vs. Zn)
Part 1: The Problem (3 minutes)
"You have 500 soil samples. You suspect they come from different geological sources, but you don't know how many or where the boundaries are. How would you group them?"
Part 2: The k-Means Algorithm (7 minutes)
"k-Means is a clustering algorithm. It tries to find 'k' groups in your data. Here's how it works:"
Step-by-step explanation with visual:
Choose k: Decide how many groups you want (e.g., k=3)
Initialize: Randomly place 3 "centroids" (cluster centers) on the plot
Assign: Each sample is assigned to the nearest centroid
Update: Recalculate the centroids as the mean of all samples assigned to them
Repeat: Go back to step 3 until the centroids stop moving
Visual: Show an animation (or sequence of images) of this process
Geological Interpretation:
"The algorithm has found 3 groups. But what do they mean? Maybe they represent different rock types, different alteration zones, or different mineralization styles. The algorithm doesn't know – that's your job to interpret."
Part 3: The Challenge of Choosing k (5 minutes)
"How do you know how many groups there are? You can try different values of k and see which makes geological sense."
Show plots with k=2, k=3, k=4, k=5
"This is where geological knowledge comes in. The algorithm can find groups, but you need to decide if those groups are meaningful."
Interactive Question:
"If you were exploring for porphyry copper deposits, what would you look for in these geochemical clusters?"
Section 3.5: Demonstration 3 – Image Analysis (10 minutes)
Setup:
Show a photograph of drill core with visible sulphide minerals (chalcopyrite, pyrite)
Ask: "How would you train a computer to identify these minerals?"
Part 1: The Challenge (3 minutes)
"A geologist can look at this core and immediately identify the sulphides. But how do you teach a computer to do that? The computer sees only pixels – numbers representing colors."
Part 2: How Image Analysis Works (5 minutes)
"Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are the most common approach. Here's the intuition:"
The image is broken into small patches
The network learns to recognize patterns in these patches (edges, textures, colors)
It combines these patterns to recognize more complex features
Eventually, it can classify the whole image or identify specific minerals
Visual: Show a simplified diagram of a CNN processing an image
Geological Application:
Automated core logging: Identify rock types, alteration, mineralization
Mineral identification: Classify minerals from thin section images
Structural analysis: Detect fractures and veins
Part 3: Limitations (2 minutes)
"Image analysis is powerful but has limitations:
It needs many labeled examples to train
It can be fooled by lighting, orientation, or image quality
It doesn't understand geology – it just recognizes patterns
Always validate the results!"
Section 3.6: Evaluating Models – The Confusion Matrix (15 minutes)
Introduction (2 minutes)
"How do you know if your model is any good? Accuracy isn't enough. You need to understand the types of errors it makes."
The Confusion Matrix (5 minutes)
Visual: A 2x2 table for a binary classification problem (e.g., mineralized vs. barren)
Definitions:
True Positive: Model predicted mineralized, and it was. Good!
True Negative: Model predicted barren, and it was. Good!
False Positive: Model predicted mineralized, but it was barren. We drilled a dud.
False Negative: Model predicted barren, but it was mineralized. We missed a deposit.
Geological Meaning (5 minutes)
"In exploration, these errors have very different costs:"
False Positive: We spend money drilling a target that turns out to be barren. Cost: $$$ but not fatal.
False Negative: We miss a deposit. Cost: Potentially the entire project, or even the company.
"In most exploration scenarios, False Negatives are much more costly than False Positives. We'd rather drill a few duds than miss a world-class deposit."
Activity: Confusion Matrix Interpretation (3 minutes)
Scenario:
"Your AI model was used to rank 100 drill targets. The company drilled the top 10. Here are the results:
6 holes were mineralized (True Positives)
4 holes were barren (False Positives)
Later, it was discovered that 2 of the undrilled targets were also mineralized (False Negatives)
The remaining 88 undrilled targets were barren (True Negatives)"
Questions:
Fill in the confusion matrix
What is the accuracy? (TP+TN)/(TP+TN+FP+FN) = (6+88)/100 = 94%
Is 94% accuracy good? Why or why not?
Which error type is more concerning here?
Discussion:
"94% accuracy sounds great, but we missed 2 out of 8 mineralized targets – that's a 25% miss rate. And we drilled 4 duds out of 10 – that's a 40% failure rate. Accuracy alone doesn't tell the whole story."
Section 3.7: Overfitting and Data Leakage (10 minutes)
Overfitting (5 minutes)
"Overfitting is when your model memorizes the training data instead of learning general patterns. It performs great on the training data but poorly on new data."
Geological Analogy:
"Imagine a student who memorizes the answers to every question in the textbook. They ace the practice test. But when they see new questions on the exam, they fail. They memorized, they didn't learn."
Visual: A graph showing training accuracy increasing while testing accuracy decreases (the classic overfitting curve)
How to Detect Overfitting:
Large gap between training and testing performance
Model performs well on training data but poorly on new data
How to Prevent Overfitting:
Use more data
Use simpler models
Use cross-validation
Regularization (a technical solution we won't cover in detail)
Data Leakage (5 minutes)
"Data leakage is when your model accidentally gets access to information it shouldn't have. It's like cheating on a test."
Geological Example:
"You want to predict gold grade from geochemical data. You include 'gold' as one of your input features. The model learns to predict gold from gold – perfect accuracy! But it's useless because you need to know the gold grade to use the model."
Common Sources of Leakage:
Including the target variable as a feature
Using future information (e.g., using drill results to predict where to drill)
Data preprocessing before splitting (e.g., normalizing with statistics from the full dataset)
Key Takeaway:
"Always ask: Would I have this information at the time I want to make a prediction? If not, it's leakage."
Module 3 Summary (5 minutes)
Key Points:
Machine learning learns patterns from data – it doesn't understand geology
Supervised learning uses labeled examples; unsupervised learning finds structure
k-NN classifies based on similarity to neighbors
k-Means finds clusters in data
Image analysis can automate tasks like core logging
The confusion matrix shows the types of errors a model makes
False Negatives are usually more costly than False Positives in exploration
Overfitting and data leakage are common pitfalls
Closing Thought:
"Machine learning is a powerful tool for finding patterns. But you, the geologist, must interpret those patterns, validate them, and make decisions. The model doesn't know what a deposit is – you do."
MODULE 4: A Complete Exploration Case Study (60 minutes)
Introduction (3 minutes)
"Now we're going to put everything together. We'll follow a realistic exploration project from start to finish, showing how AI and geological expertise work together."
The Scenario: "The Hidden Porphyry Project" (5 minutes)
Background:
You work for a junior exploration company
You have a large land package (500 km²) in a known porphyry copper belt
The area is partially covered by post-mineral cover
Previous exploration found some geochemical anomalies but no economic mineralization
The company has limited funds – they can only drill 5 targets
Available Data:
Regional magnetic survey (200m line spacing)
Regional radiometric survey (K, U, Th)
Historical soil geochemistry (5,000 samples, 30 elements)
Geological map (1:50,000)
10 old drill holes (mostly shallow, some mineralized intersections)
The Goal:
"Rank your targets and decide where to drill. Use AI to help, but remember – you're the geologist. You make the final decision."
Step 1: Understanding the Geological Problem (5 minutes)
The Senior Geologist's Model:
"I believe there's potential for a buried porphyry system. Based on my experience in this belt, I'd look for:
A magnetic low (magnetite destruction during alteration)
A potassic radiometric signature (high K, high Th/K ratio)
Cu-Mo-Au geochemical anomalies
Proximity to major structures
Favorable host rocks (intermediate to felsic intrusions)"
Interactive Question:
"Based on your geological knowledge, does this model make sense? What would you add or change?"
Step 2: Data Preparation (10 minutes)
Show the Data Issues:
Geochemistry:
Soil data is "clinched" along roads
Different detection limits for different elements
Some samples missing Mo (not analyzed)
Values below detection limit reported as "<2 ppm"
Geophysics:
Magnetic data is good quality
Radiometric data has some noise from cultural features (roads, buildings)
Geology:
Map is good but has some unit boundaries that are uncertain
Discussion:
"What would you do about each of these issues? Turn to your neighbor and discuss for 2 minutes."
Instructor's Approach:
Log-transform geochemical data
Impute missing Mo values using k-NN
Replace censored values with detection limit/2
Mask out cultural noise in radiometric data
Accept the geological map as-is but note uncertainties
Step 3: Feature Engineering (10 minutes)
What is Feature Engineering?
"Feature engineering is creating new data layers that represent the geological concepts you care about. It's translating geological knowledge into data."
Examples:
From Geophysics:
Magnetic low: Invert the magnetic data (high values become low)
Potassic signature: Calculate Th/K ratio from radiometric data
From Geochemistry:
Cu anomaly: Calculate a threshold (e.g., >95th percentile)
Cu-Mo-Au association: Create a combined index
From Geology:
Distance to nearest fault: Calculate Euclidean distance
Host rock favorability: Assign a score based on rock type
From Multiple Sources:
Combined favorability: Weighted sum of magnetic, radiometric, and geochemical layers
Visual: Show a map with several feature layers
Interactive Question:
"What other features would you create based on the geological model?"
Step 4: Building the Model (10 minutes)
The Approach:
Problem Type: Binary classification (mineralized vs. barren)
Labels: Known mineralized intersections from old drill holes (1) and barren intersections (0)
Features: All the layers we created (magnetic, radiometric, geochemical, distance to fault, host rock)
Training the Model:
Split data: 70% training, 30% testing
Use a Random Forest classifier (we haven't covered this, but it's an ensemble of decision trees – it's robust and interpretable)
Train the model on the training data
Important Note:
"We're using the old drill holes as our labeled examples. This is a small dataset, so we need to be careful about overfitting."
Visual: A diagram showing the training process
Step 5: Making Predictions (5 minutes)
The Prospectivity Map:
Apply the trained model to every pixel in the project area
Each pixel gets a probability of mineralization (0-100%)
Visual: Show a prospectivity map with hot (red) and cold (blue) zones
Interpretation:
"The map shows three high-probability zones. But remember – this is a statistical prediction, not a geological guarantee."
Step 6: Geological Validation (10 minutes)
Overlay the Geology:
Show the prospectivity map with the geological map overlaid
Target A:
High probability (85%)
Located in favorable host rock
Near a major fault
Coincident with a Cu-Mo-Au soil anomaly
Geological assessment: Excellent target
Target B:
High probability (75%)
Located in a different rock type (less favorable)
No obvious structural control
Weak geochemical anomaly
Geological assessment: Questionable target
Target C:
High probability (90%)
Located in the middle of a lake
Geological assessment: Artifact – the model doesn't know it's a lake!
Discussion:
"Which targets would you drill? Why? Turn to your neighbor and discuss."
Step 7: The Decision – Role-Play Activity (10 minutes)
Setup:
"Let's simulate a real exploration meeting. I'll assign you roles:
Chief Geologist (argues for geological merit)
Data Scientist (argues for model performance)

"I want to start with a question that might make some of you uncomfortable: In the next 10 years, will you be replaced by a computer?
Pause for effect

It's about technology changing what you do and how you do it. And the geologists who embrace this change will be the ones who thrive.
Let me give you a personal example. When I started in exploration, we did everything by hand. We plotted geochemical data on paper maps. We contour lines manually. We spent weeks on tasks that now take minutes. Did that make us worse geologists? No. It freed us up to focus on the geology – the interpretation, the thinking, the decision-making.
AI is the same. It's a tool that handles the repetitive, the complex, and the data-intensive tasks. Your job is to be the geologist – to understand the geology, to interpret the results, and to make the decisions. Let's learn how."
Slide 1.7: Traditional Programming vs. ML
Speaker Notes:
"Let me explain the fundamental difference between traditional programming and machine learning.
In traditional programming – think of writing a formula in Excel – you provide the rules. You say, 'If copper is greater than 1,000 ppm, then flag this sample as anomalous.' The computer follows your rule and gives you an answer.
But geology is too complex for simple rules. What if the copper anomaly is in a different rock type? What if it's associated with a different element? What if the threshold should be different in different areas? You'd need hundreds of rules, and they'd all be wrong somewhere.
Machine learning flips this. Instead of giving the computer rules, we give it examples. We show it 500 samples that are mineralized and 500 that are barren. We say, 'Figure out the pattern.' The computer finds the rules itself – and those rules can be far more complex than any human could write.
Here's the key: the computer doesn't understand geology. It finds statistical patterns. It doesn't know what a deposit is. It doesn't know what a fault is. It just knows that certain combinations of numbers tend to go together.
Our job – your job – is to interpret those patterns geologically. To look at what the model found and ask, 'Does this make sense? Is this geologically reasonable?' That's where you come in."

MODULE 2: Detailed Teaching Content
Slide 2.9: The Golden Rule
Speaker Notes:
"Let me tell you a story that illustrates why data quality matters so much.
I was once working on a project in South America. We had a large soil geochemistry dataset – 10,000 samples, 50 elements. We were building a model to predict where to drill. We spent weeks preparing the data, and we were feeling pretty good about it.
Then, right before we ran the model, one of the senior geologists said, 'Wait. Let me look at the raw data one more time.'
She found that one of the labs had reported values in different units than the other lab. We had merged the datasets without realizing it. Half our data was in ppm, half in ppb. A factor of 1,000 error.
If we had run the model without catching that, the results would have been completely wrong. We would have been drilling in the wrong places. We would have wasted millions of dollars.
This is the Golden Rule: Garbage In, Garbage Out. If your data is bad, your model will be bad. No algorithm can fix bad data. This is why data preparation is so important – and why your geological knowledge is so valuable. You know what the data should look like. You can spot when something is wrong."
Slide 2.19: Spatial Bias
Speaker Notes:
"I want to show you a map that illustrates one of the most common and most dangerous problems in exploration data.
Show map with sample points clustered along roads
This is a soil geochemistry map from a real project. Each dot is a sample location. Notice anything?
Pause for responses
Yes – they're all along roads. This is what we call 'clinched' data. The geologists sampled where it was easy to access – along roads and tracks. They didn't sample the areas between the roads because it was too difficult.
Now, imagine you train an AI model on this data. What will it learn? It will learn to find deposits near roads. It will become an expert at that. But what about the deposit that's 5 kilometers from the nearest road? The model has no examples to learn from. It might miss it entirely.
This is spatial bias. It's a huge problem in exploration because we almost always sample where it's easy. And it's a problem that a pure data scientist might miss – but a geologist would spot immediately.
So what do we do about it? First, be aware. Second, test your model on spatially separate data – not just random splits. Third, if you can, collect data in the underrepresented areas. And fourth, always interpret your results with this bias in mind."
Slide 2.20: Data Leakage
Speaker Notes:
"Data leakage is one of the most common and most damaging mistakes in applied machine learning. And it's subtle – it's easy to do without realizing it.
Here's the concept: Data leakage is when your model accidentally gets access to information it shouldn't have. Information that wouldn't be available when you actually use the model to make predictions.
Let me give you a geological example.
Imagine you want to build a model that predicts gold grade from geochemical data. You have a dataset with 50 elements analyzed for each sample, plus the gold grade. You decide to use all 50 elements as input features.
But wait – gold is one of those 50 elements. You're using gold to predict gold. The model will achieve perfect accuracy on your test set. But it's useless because in the real world, you wouldn't know the gold grade before you drill.
This is leakage. You've leaked the answer into the features.
Another example: You want to predict where to drill next. You train a model using all your drill hole data – including the final grade of each hole. Then you apply the model to undrilled areas. But you've trained the model on the answers. It's learned to predict grade from grade.
The correct approach is to only use information that would be available before you drill. Geophysics, surface geochemistry, geological mapping. Not the drill results.
The rule is simple: Always ask, 'Would I have this information at the time I want to make a prediction?' If the answer is no, it's leakage."

Slide 3.6-3.10: Rock Detective Demo
Speaker Notes:
Slide 3.6:
"Let's do a demonstration. I'm going to show you a scatter plot with two axes: Zr on the x-axis and TiO2 on the y-axis. These are two elements that are useful for classifying igneous rocks.
I've plotted samples from three known rock types: Basalt in blue, Andesite in green, and Rhyolite in red. Each point is a sample where we know the rock type from field mapping or petrography."
Slide 3.7:
"Now, I'm going to add a new sample – the big yellow star. Based on its position on this plot, what rock type do you think it is?
Turn to your neighbor. You have 30 seconds. Be ready to justify your answer."
After 30 seconds:
"Who wants to share? What did you decide?"
Students will say "Andesite" because it's closest to the green points.
"Exactly! You said it's Andesite because it's closest to the other Andesite samples. You looked at its neighbors and classified it based on the most common class among them. That's exactly how the k-Nearest Neighbors algorithm works."
Slide 3.8:
"Let me show you what the algorithm does. It draws a circle around the unknown sample – this circle contains the 'k' nearest neighbors.
If k=1, we look at only the closest neighbor. If that neighbor is Andesite, we predict Andesite.
If k=3, we look at the 3 closest neighbors. We take a vote. If 2 are Andesite and 1 is Rhyolite, we predict Andesite.
If k=5, we look at 5 neighbors. The majority wins."
Slide 3.9:
"The choice of k matters. If k is too small, you're sensitive to noise – one unusual sample could change the prediction. If k is too large, you might include samples from other rock types and blur the boundaries.
In practice, we try different values of k and see which works best. This is called 'hyperparameter tuning.'"
Slide 3.10:
"Now here's the power of machine learning. This plot has 2 dimensions – Zr and TiO2. But imagine it had 50 dimensions – one for each element in a geochemical analysis. The computer can find neighbors in 50-dimensional space. That's impossible for us to visualize, but the math works the same way.
This is why machine learning is so powerful for geochemistry. It can find patterns in high-dimensional data that we could never see."
Slide 3.11-3.16: k-Means Clustering Demo
Speaker Notes:
Slide 3.11:
"Now let's look at a different type of problem. This time, we have no labels. We have 500 soil samples, and we've measured two elements: Cu and Zn. But we don't know what rock type they came from. We don't know if they're mineralized or barren.
Our question is: Do these samples form natural groups? Can we find structure in the data without any labels?
This is called clustering, and it's an example of unsupervised learning."
Slide 3.12:
"The k-Means algorithm is one of the most common clustering methods. Here's how it works.
Step 1: We decide how many clusters we want. Let's say k=3. We randomly place 3 'centroids' on the plot – these are the initial guesses for the cluster centers."
Slide 3.13:
"Step 2: Each sample is assigned to the nearest centroid. The samples assigned to the same centroid form a cluster."
Slide 3.14:
"Step 3: We recalculate the centroids. Each centroid moves to the center of all the samples assigned to it."
Slide 3.15:
"Step 4: We repeat steps 2 and 3. Samples are reassigned, centroids move, until the centroids stop moving. At that point, the algorithm has converged."
Slide 3.16:
"Here's the challenge: How do you know how many clusters there are? We tried k=3, but what if the real answer is k=2 or k=4?
Show plots with different k values
There's no single right answer. This is where geological knowledge comes in. The algorithm can find groups, but you need to decide if those groups are meaningful.
If you're exploring for porphyry copper deposits, you might look at these clusters and ask: Do they represent different alteration zones? Different rock types? Different mineralization styles? The algorithm doesn't know – that's your job."
Slide 3.21-3.23: Confusion Matrix
Speaker Notes:
Slide 3.21:
"How do you know if your model is any good? You might think accuracy is the answer, but it's not that simple.
Let me introduce the confusion matrix. This is a 2x2 table that shows the types of errors a model makes.
Show the table
For a binary classification problem – say, mineralized vs. barren – there are four possible outcomes:
True Positive: The model predicted mineralized, and it was mineralized. Correct!
True Negative: The model predicted barren, and it was barren. Correct!
False Positive: The model predicted mineralized, but it was barren. Wrong!
False Negative: The model predicted barren, but it was mineralized. Wrong!"
Slide 3.22:
"In exploration, these two types of errors have very different costs.
A False Positive means we drilled a target that turned out to be barren. We wasted money – maybe a few hundred thousand dollars. Painful, but not fatal.
A False Negative means we missed a deposit. We didn't drill a target that was actually mineralized. That could mean missing a world-class deposit worth billions of dollars. That could mean the end of the project – or the company.
In most exploration scenarios, False Negatives are much more costly than False Positives. We'd rather drill a few duds than miss a deposit. But the exact trade-off depends on the situation. In a greenfields program, you might accept more False Positives. In a resource definition program, you might be more concerned about False Negatives."
Slide 3.23:
"Let's do an activity. Here's a scenario:
Your AI model was used to rank 100 drill targets. The company drilled the top 10. Here are the results:
6 holes were mineralized (True Positives)
4 holes were barren (False Positives)
Later, it was discovered that 2 of the undrilled targets were also mineralized (False Negatives)
The remaining 88 undrilled targets were barren (True Negatives)
Turn to your neighbor. Fill in the confusion matrix. Then answer these questions:
What is the accuracy? (TP+TN)/(Total) = (6+88)/100 = 94%
Is 94% accuracy good? Why or why not?
Which error type is more concerning here?
You have 3 minutes."
After 3 minutes:
"Let's discuss. What did you find?"
*Guide discussion to the key insight: 94% accuracy sounds great, but we missed 2 out of 8 mineralized targets – that's a 25% miss rate. And 40% of the holes we drilled were barren. Accuracy alone doesn't tell the whole story. You need to understand the types of errors."
Slide 3.24-3.25: Overfitting
Speaker Notes:
Slide 3.24:
"Overfitting is one of the most common problems in machine learning. It happens when your model memorizes the training data instead of learning general patterns.
Show the graph
This graph shows what happens. The training accuracy keeps going up – the model gets better and better on the data it was trained on. But the testing accuracy – performance on new data – starts to decrease. The model has memorized the training data, but it can't generalize to new data."
Slide 3.25:
"Here's a geological analogy. Imagine a student who memorizes the answers to every question in the textbook. They ace the practice test. But when they see new questions on the exam, they fail. They memorized, they didn't learn.
Machine learning models can do the same thing. If you have too few examples, or too complex a model, it will memorize the training data. It will perform perfectly on the data it's seen, but poorly on new data.
How do you prevent overfitting?
Use more data
Use simpler models
Use cross-validation
Always test on data the model hasn't seen
And remember: if your model performs perfectly on the training data but poorly on the test data, it's overfitting."
